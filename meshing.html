Delaunay triangulation, one point at a time

Click to add a point. No vertex may lie inside the circumcircle of a triangle: that is the Delaunay rule (Delaunay, 1934).

  • Bowyer (1981), Watson (1981): find the triangles whose circumcircle holds the new point. Remove them. The hole is a star-shaped cavity. Connect its boundary to the point.
  • Lawson (1977): split the triangle that holds the point into three. Then flip each edge that fails the circle test, until none fails.
  • Both give the same mesh. Of all triangulations of these points, the Delaunay one has the largest smallest angle.
  • Voronoi: join the circumcentres of neighbour triangles. It is the dual graph. Each cell holds the points nearest to one vertex.

Advancing front: build from the boundary in

The front starts as the boundary edges. Take the shortest front edge. Put an ideal point above it, or use a near front vertex. Add the triangle. Update the front. Repeat until the front is empty (Lo, 1985; Peraire et al., 1987).

The front makes near-equilateral triangles in rows that follow the boundary. Its worst triangles are where two fronts meet. Delaunay refinement has a bounded worst angle but a wider spread. At this size the front wins on mean γ; at h ≥ 0.6 it does not. Gmsh “Frontal-Delaunay” (the 2-D default) mixes both: it places points like a front and connects them with Delaunay (Rebay, 1993).

Quality: Delaunay refinement with an angle bound

Delaunay triangulation alone keeps skinny triangles. Ruppert's algorithm (1995) removes them. Raise the minimum angle and watch the mesh change.

Why 20.7°

ρ = R / ℓmin = 1 / (2 sin θmin)  ·  ρ > √2 ⇔ θmin < arcsin(1/(2√2)) = 20.7°

  • Bad triangle (smallest angle < α): insert its circumcentre. It is far from all vertices (the circle is empty), so new edges are not short.
  • Encroached segment (a vertex inside its diametral circle): split it at the midpoint. If a circumcentre would encroach a segment, split the segment instead.
  • The guarantee: split only triangles with ρ above a bound B ≥ √2. Then each new edge is longer than the edge that caused it, so the algorithm stops. That gives α up to 20.7°. Ruppert (1995) proved it for inputs with no angle under 90°; Shewchuk (2002) relaxed this to 60°. Chew's 1989 method gives 30° for uniform meshes.
  • In practice Triangle succeeds up to about 33° and usually does not stop above 34° (Shewchuk). Drag the bound past 34°: this demo stops at a cap of 6,000 points.

3-D: tetrahedra and slivers

      
A sliver has four good edges but almost no volume: the four vertices are nearly in one plane. Delaunay refinement in 3-D bounds the radius–edge ratio, and slivers pass that test.   • Why slivers hurt FEM: a dihedral angle near 0° or 180° makes the element stiffness matrix ill-conditioned. For the flattened tet, κ grows like 1/V² (its largest eigenvalue like 1/V). The gradient interpolation also gets worse: this is the maximum angle condition (Babuška & Aziz 1976; Křížek 1992). Iterative solvers then need more iterations.
    • 
      
  • No sliver problem in 2-D: a triangle with good edges cannot be flat. In 3-D, the radius–edge ratio of a flat sliver is 1/√2 = 0.71, better than TetGen's default bound of 2.0.
    • 
    

    

      
TetGen (Si, 2015)Delaunay refinement + optimisation
-q bounds the radius–edge ratio (default 2.0) and, as an option, the minimum dihedral angle. -O then flips edges and faces and smooths vertices; its default target is a maximum dihedral angle of 165°.

      
Netgen (Schöberl, 1997)advancing front + optimisation
Rule-based front placement, then edge/face swaps and smoothing. Gmsh runs it with Mesh.OptimizeNetgen = 1; Gmsh “Frontal” 3-D (4) is Netgen.

      
HXT (Marot et al. 2019, 2020)parallel Delaunay + optimisation
A parallel Delaunay kernel, then edge removal, Laplacian smoothing and the “growing SPR cavity” operator. Gmsh Mesh.Algorithm3D = 10.

      
Gmsh optimiserdefault on
Mesh.Optimize = 1 improves tetrahedra with quality below Mesh.OptimizeThreshold (default 0.3). Check with Plugin(AnalyseMeshQuality) or the SICN histogram.

      
Sliver exudation (Cheng et al. 2000)weighted Delaunay
Give each vertex a small weight. A weighted Delaunay mesh then has no slivers below a (very small) proven dihedral bound. A theory result; practical codes use flips and smoothing.

    

  


  
  

    
Adaptive refinement: let the solution choose the mesh

      
Solve, estimate the error of each element, mark the worst, refine, repeat. The test case is the L-shaped domain. Its re-entrant corner makes the gradient infinite, like a metal corner in EM.

u = r2/3 sin(2θ/3)  ·  |u|H¹² = 1.8362  ·  P1: uniform error ∝ h2/3 ∝ N−1/3; adaptive ∝ N−1/2 (optimal)

    
    
      
  • Estimate: the residual estimator sums the jumps of the normal flux across the edges of T: ηT² = ½Σ hE²[∂nuh]² (Babuška & Rheinboldt 1978). ZZ averages the gradient at the nodes and measures the gap to the element gradient (Zienkiewicz & Zhu 1987). The effectivity η/error shows how honest the estimate is: near 1.0 for ZZ here, about 3.4 for the residual on the adaptive meshes (its bound has an unknown constant).
    • 
      
  • Mark (Dörfler 1996): mark the fewest elements that hold the fraction θ of the total η². Small θ: few elements per pass, a lean mesh, more passes. θ = 1 is uniform.
    • 
      
  • Refine: newest-vertex bisection (Sewell 1972; Mitchell 1991). The closure step bisects neighbours too, so there are no hanging nodes. Every element is similar to one of a few shapes of the first mesh, so the angles stay bounded. The optimal rate is proven (Binev, Dahmen & DeVore 2004; Stevenson 2007).

HFSS adaptive passesstop on ΔS
Solve at one adapt frequency. Estimate the error per tetrahedron. Refine a fraction of them (Ansys training: 30% per pass by default). Stop when ΔS, the largest change of any S-parameter between two passes, is below the target (default 0.02) for the set number of converged passes. ΔS watches the output, not the field error.

      
Palace AMRconfig["Model"]["Refinement"]
A ZZ-type estimator on the recovered B and D fields (after Nicaise 2005). Dörfler marking with UpdateFraction (default 0.7). Stop at Tol (default 0.01) or MaxIts (default 0: no AMR). Nonconformal is true by default, with MaxNCLevels = 1; conformal refinement is for simplex meshes only.

    

    

      
Conformal: red–green refinement

        
        
Red: split the marked element in four. Green: bisect each neighbour to the new midpoint. Every node is a true vertex. (The demo above uses newest-vertex bisection; the result is also conforming.)

      
Nonconformal: hanging nodes

        
        
Only the marked element splits. The midpoints on the neighbours' edges hang (rings). The solver must constrain those DOFs to the coarse edge. It works for any element type, but the constraints add coupling.

    

    
An observed case, not a general rule

      
In one of Ben's Palace models, nonconformal AMR on a large mesh made the wave-port mode solve pick a spurious mode. If a port mode looks wrong after AMR, check the port field plots. On a tetrahedral mesh, also try "Nonconformal": false.
